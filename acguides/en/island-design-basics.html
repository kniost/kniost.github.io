Island design basics: plan first, then grab the shovel

Planning, terraforming, paths, scenery, and a few helpers that save you time

Lots of players start digging the moment they get the Island Designer app, only to realize halfway through that a river blocks the way or a house is in the wrong spot — and then they have to tear it all down. Redoing work is the biggest time sink in island design: moving a building costs Bells and takes a day, and filling in a river goes one tile at a time. So before you pick up a shovel, take some time to sketch out the whole island on paper or on your phone.

There's really just one rule for the order: place buildings first, then terraform, and save paths, flowers, and furniture for last. If you're unsure about a layout, try it out freely on a Slumber Island first, and only start work on your real island once you're happy with it.

01Map out your island

Once your island hits 3 stars and you've seen K.K. Slider's concert, Tom Nook installs the Island Designer app on your NookPhone the next day. Grass and dirt paths are available from the start; stone, brick, and wooden paths, along with the waterscaping and cliff construction permits, have to be bought with Nook Miles at the Nook Stop in Resident Services.

Before you start, roughly divide your island into areas and decide what goes in each:

The entrance area is the first thing you see when you leave the airport — it's where visitors form their first impression of your island.

The residential area holds your house and your villagers' homes.

The shopping area holds Nook's Cranny, Able Sisters, and the museum. Line them up along one street so they're easy to visit in one go.

The nature area is for orchards, flower fields, woods, and the beach.

Resident Services, the airport, the pier, and the river mouths can't be moved, so treat them as anchors and arrange everything else around them.

Tom Nook can relocate buildings for you: 30,000 Bells to move your own house, and 50,000 Bells each time for Nook's Cranny, the museum, or a villager's house. Only one building can be moved at a time, and it's finished the next day. Moving is expensive and slow, so lock in building positions before terraforming — otherwise you may end up tearing up finished terrain just to shift a house.

You can draw your plan in an online island designer, or use Island Planner in the Nook Circle iOS app to lay out rivers, cliffs, and buildings on your phone first and check whether all your paths actually connect.

02Terraforming essentials

Clear first, then build; big shapes before small details

You can't have trees, rocks, furniture, or buildings right next to cliffs and rivers — anything adjacent blocks construction. So clear out the area you're reworking first, then shape the overall outline of rivers and cliffs. Only once the outline is set should you lay paths and plant flowers.

Waterfalls need cliffs on both sides

For a waterfall to form where a river flows over a cliff, that tile must have cliff on both sides. Conversely, if you want to remove a section of cliff that has a waterfall, you'll need to fill in that stretch of river first.

You can build 10 bridges and 10 inclines

There's a cap, so plan carefully, especially on islands with lots of elevation changes. Every level needs a way up and down — don't trap yourself on a cliff.

Coconut trees go on sand

Coconut trees only grow on sand. Lay a sand path with the Island Designer and you can plant them near grassy areas instead of cramming them all onto the beach.

TipPress L to snap your character to the grid; hold L to walk in one direction only. Hold it while digging rivers, building cliffs, laying paths, or putting up fences to get perfectly straight lines.

03Paths and custom designs

Paths are the backbone of your island. Make main paths wider — two or three tiles — to connect each area; side paths can be one tile wide, winding into flower fields and woods.

Besides the built-in paths, you can lay custom tiles with Custom Designs. People have made stone walkways, boardwalks, flower bed borders, crosswalks, and more, and you'll find tons of ready-made design codes online. For how to scan them in, see "Pro Designs: turn photos into clothing and wall art."

Watch the edges when using custom tiles. Where built-in paths meet grass, the corners round off automatically, but custom tiles don't — the edges end in a hard, straight line that looks stiff. Pick designs with a grass transition built in, or soften the edges with bushes or flowers.

Also, don't pave over everything. Leaving some grass and dirt keeps your island looking natural.

04Building scenes with furniture

Furniture scattered randomly around the island looks messy; arranged in little scenes, it looks so much better. Pick a theme, choose a focal point, and frame it with fences, bushes, and flowers. A few tricks for arranging:

Use one furniture series as the base for each area — say log, iron garden, bamboo, or marble. Pick a set and build on it, and the style stays cohesive.

Put shorter items on the side facing the path and taller ones in back. Low in front, high in back gives the scene depth from the path and keeps the back row visible.

Leave a one-tile walkway into each scene so visitors can step in and take photos.

Check your scenes at night, too. Once streetlamps, lanterns, and campfires light up, your island takes on a whole different look.

The stall: the easiest scenery piece

The stall DIY recipe costs 2,000 Nook Miles at the Nook Stop, and each one takes just 12 wood. Its awning can be swapped for your own design, so the same stall can become a fruit stand, a coffee stand, or a night-market snack stall — or a free-swap table. The simple panel sold at Nook's Cranny also takes custom designs, perfect for signs, menus, and street signs.

05Three time-saving helpers

Resetti's cleanup service

Clearing an area before a makeover is the most tedious part, so let Resetti handle it. He can clear all the furniture, flowers, and placed designs from a whole beach, the whole island, or an area you choose in one go, and you can either toss them or send them to storage. The first time is free; after that it's 60,000 Bells per day, with unlimited use that day once you've paid. He won't touch weeds, trees, bushes, or anything buried.

After Resetti's cleanup, everything on the ground is gone in one go (Image: Nintendo)

9,000 storage slots — flowers and trees included

Storage can be expanded to 9,000 slots, and you can put flowers and trees straight into it. No more replanting them one by one somewhere else for safekeeping — just take them back out and replant once the terraforming is done.

Slumber Islands

When you go to sleep in your bed, Luna can take you to a Slumber Island. Each player can create up to 3. You can terraform freely there, decorate with any furniture registered in your catalog, change the time and weather, and even have friends come help. Nothing from the dream comes back to your real island, which makes Slumber Islands ideal for testing layouts: try your ideas there, and once you like the result, recreate it at home. Visiting Slumber Islands requires a Nintendo Switch Online membership.

06Common beginner mistakes

Not leaving room for new villagers. Until you have 10 villagers, new houses can only go up where there's open space. When planning your residential area, account for villagers who haven't moved in yet.

Choppy cliffs. Jagged cliffs look bad and are a pain to walk around. Keep the edges smooth and leave enough space at corners for inclines.

Trees packed too tightly. Trees planted too close together won't grow, and too many can drag down your island rating. For a forest feel, fill in with bushes and flowers.

Redoing the whole island at once. Work on one area at a time and finish it before starting the next, so your island doesn't look like a construction site for weeks.
